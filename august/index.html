How much Python does a data analyst actually need?

Thirteen sessions are done. This page says what they covered, what a data analyst will never use, and the six sessions that close the gap before NumPy starts.

The short answer

A data analyst uses roughly a third of what a general Python course teaches. The rest belongs to whoever is building the software, not to whoever is reading the data.

Of the 15.3 hours delivered, about 2.9 hours went to topics an analyst will not use. One whole session on the evolution of computers, plus the recursion half of D13 and the reduce() half of D12.

That is not a disaster — it is roughly one session. But three things that matter far more have not been taught at all: slicing, list comprehension, and reading a file. Nothing in pandas makes sense without the first two.

What is missing

Confirmed absent from all thirteen recordings. The four marked critical block everything that comes after them.

List slicingcritical
lst[1:4], lst[:3], lst[::-1]

Zero mentions across all 13 sessions. This is how you take a subset of anything in Python.

List comprehensioncritical
[x*2 for x in nums]

Zero mentions. The single most-used construct in day-to-day analyst code.

The split

Same language, two different jobs. A full-stack developer writes the software; an analyst reads what the software produced. The overlap is smaller than most syllabuses assume.

The plan from here

Six sessions close every gap. NumPy starts on the seventh.

D14
Strings for cleaning

strip, lower, upper, replace, split, join, f-strings. Every example a messy name, phone number or date.

Finish what D13 started, then stop. No regex yet.

D15
Slicing and negative indexing

lst[1:4], lst[:3], lst[-1], lst[::-1] — on lists and on strings, same rule both times.

The gap that blocks everything downstream.

D16
List comprehension

Rewrite three loops they already wrote in D11 as one-liners, side by side.

The highest-value session in the whole Python block.

D17
Dictionaries at depth

Nested dicts, a list of dicts as a table, looping over items() to build a summary.

This is the shape a DataFrame actually is.

D18
while, break, continue, and import

while and the two keywords in 25 minutes. Then pip, import, and the alias convention.

Clears the last two gaps in one session.

D19
Files, and the move to the notebook

Open a CSV with plain Python and read it line by line. Then switch the class to Jupyter or Colab.

They must feel the pain before pandas removes it.

D20
NumPy begins

Arrays, and why a list is the wrong tool once there are a million numbers.

Python block closes here.

One decision to make in D19. The class is on PyCharm. PyCharm is a tool for people who build software — it runs a whole file and prints at the end. An analyst works one step at a time, looking at the table after each step, which is what Jupyter and Colab are for.

The longer the switch waits, the more it costs. D19 is the natural moment, because that is the session where they first load a file and want to look at it.
